# How can we ensure our citizen observatory is digitally inclusive?

## Why is it relevant?

Digital technologies are at the heart of most citizen science and citizen observatory initiatives — from the sensors used to collect environmental data, to the apps and platforms through which participants contribute observations and connect with one another. Yet the same technologies that make it possible to scale up participation and reach new audiences can, if not carefully considered, quietly exclude the very people a project most needs to include. The gap between those who can, or are willing, to use technology and those who cannot or do not want to is a persistent challenge in citizen science — and one that sits at the intersection of access, skills, knowledge, and attitude.

Understanding why some people are digitally excluded, and what it would take to include them, requires more than providing a smartphone app or a set of instructions. It demands a systematic look at the competencies — the skills, knowledge, and attitudes — of both the participants we hope to engage and the project leaders and facilitators who design and run the initiative. Without this understanding, it is not possible to systematically measure, and consequently enhance, the conditions for inclusion. And without that foundation, the risk is that citizen science initiatives reproduce the very inequalities they aim to address.

Digital inclusion is not a purely technical problem. It is shaped by social, cultural, economic, and political context; by attitudes towards science, technology, and participation; by the presence or absence of trust; and by whether a project's design reflects the genuine needs and circumstances of the communities it seeks to involve. Addressing it well means embedding inclusive thinking from the very start — in how technologies are chosen, how tasks are designed, how training is provided, and how participants' contributions are recognised and valued.
